## Why is the advertised rate so low?

Usually one or more of these:

- **Points:** you'd pay a big upfront fee (often thousands of dollars) to get that rate.
- **Adjustable rate:** the low rate lasts a few years and then can rise.
- **Best-case assumptions:** top credit score, lots of equity, a smaller loan, a primary home.
- **A shorter term:** a 15-year rate shown next to a 30-year payment example.

## How do I spot hidden points or fees?

- **Compare the rate to the APR.** The APR includes most costs. A big gap between the two means fees or points.
- **Read the fine print** for "points," "adjustable," and the assumptions behind the example.
- **Ask for a Loan Estimate.** It's a standard form, so you can line it up against other offers. Look at page 2, Section A (origination charges and points).
- **Check who sent it.** Some mailers look like they're from your current lender or the government when they aren't.

## How do I compare it fairly?

Get Loan Estimates from two or three lenders **on the same day, for the same loan**, and compare the total of the rate plus costs. Here's [how to tell if your Loan Estimate is a good deal](/answers/how-to-tell-if-your-loan-estimate-is-a-good-deal).

## Example scenario (illustrative)

A homeowner in Denver gets a mailer with a rate a full point below what she's been quoted. The fine print shows it includes about two points upfront and assumes a 780 credit score and 40% equity. With her real numbers on a Loan Estimate, the offer is about the same as the quotes she already has, with more cash due at closing.
