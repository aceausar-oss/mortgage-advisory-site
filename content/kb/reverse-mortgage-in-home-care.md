## How can the money be used for care?

- **In-home care aides** or visiting nurses
- **Home modifications:** grab bars, a walk-in shower, a first-floor bedroom, ramps
- **Paying a family caregiver**, with a proper written agreement
- **Covering living costs** so savings can go toward care

A **line of credit** works well because care needs change: draw only what's needed, when it's needed.

## When is it a good fit?

When the client wants to **age in place**, the home can be made safe, and care needs can be met at home for years, not months. It can also be a bridge while long-term care insurance benefits begin.

## When is it a poor fit?

When the client is likely to need **assisted living or a nursing home soon**. On a HECM, the loan generally comes due if the last borrower is away from the home for **more than 12 months in a row**. If a spouse or co-borrower still lives there, the loan usually doesn't come due. See [assisted living and reverse mortgages](/answers/reverse-mortgage-assisted-living).

## What about Medicaid?

Loan proceeds aren't income, but money held into the next month can count as an asset for needs-based programs. Draw as needed from the line instead of taking a lump sum. See [Medicaid, Social Security, and Medicare](/answers/reverse-mortgage-medicaid-social-security).

## Our take

Care decisions are emotional, and families often wait until there's a crisis. The best time to set up a line of credit is before care is needed, while the client is healthy and has time to plan. Bring the family in early; I'm happy to explain the options to everyone at once.
